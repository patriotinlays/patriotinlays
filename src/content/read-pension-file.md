# How to Read a Revolutionary War Pension File, Page by Page

A pension file is often the single richest record for a Revolutionary War ancestor: service, residence, age, sometimes a marriage or a page torn from a family Bible. It is also confusing on first contact, because the papers are not in a logical order and the envelope uses a shorthand that few people explain. This guide walks through one file from the outside in.

## Step 1: Find the file

The pension and bounty-land files are microfilm publication M804, and the National Archives Catalog holds them as NAID 300022. [[claim:nara-amrev-m804]]
The same files are on FamilySearch, free with a login. [[claim:nara-amrev-familysearch]]
NARA also publishes volunteer transcriptions of digitized pension pages as bulk downloads. [[claim:nara-bulk]]
The selected records from the bigger files were also filmed as a separate, shorter publication, Selected Records From Revolutionary War Pension and Bounty-Land-Warrant Application Files. [[claim:m805-selected]] That publication is Microcopy 805 (M805 for short). [[claim:m805-number]]
If you would rather have copies from the National Archives itself, its NATF Form 85 orders copies of federal military pension files for service from 1775. [[claim:nara-natf85-order]] An order can be for a pre-Civil War file, such as a Revolutionary War file; see [the National Archives' ordering page](https://www.archives.gov/veterans/military-service-records/pre-ww-1-records). [[claim:nara-natf85-revwar]]
The files number around eighty thousand. [[claim:nara-amrev-80k]]
The microfilm photographed more than 2.3 million documents in more than 83,000 files. [[claim:nps-scale]]

Two limits are worth knowing before you search. The files hold only pensions granted or paid by the federal government; states paid pensions too, and those records are elsewhere. [[claim:prologue-federal-only]]
A War Department fire on November 8, 1800 apparently destroyed the applications filed before it. [[claim:m804-fire]]

## Step 2: Decode the envelope

| On the envelope | Meaning |
|---|---|
| S + number | A survivor's file: the veteran himself applied [[claim:m804-s]] |
| W + number | A widow's file [[claim:m804-w]] |
| R + number | Rejected; usually only the latest application in the file was rejected [[claim:m804-r-latest]] |
| "Former Widow" | The widow remarried; her later surname appears too [[claim:m804-former-widow]] |
| "Dis. No Papers" | A pre-1800 disability pension whose original papers are gone [[claim:m804-dis]] |
| B. L. Wt. + numbers | Bounty-land warrant file [[claim:m804-blwt]] |
| "55" at the end of a B. L. Wt. number | Warrant issued under the bounty-land act of 1855 [[claim:m804-55]] |
| A star by the surname | A short file of 10 pages or fewer [[claim:m804-star]] |

An R file can still hold an approved application that was granted before the last one was rejected. [[claim:m804-r-approved]]

## Step 3: Read in the right order

Within files, the records are unarranged. [[claim:m804-unarranged]]
The National Park Service gives the same warning: papers within a pension file are not arranged in any particular order. [[claim:nps-order]]
In files longer than 10 pages, the filmers put the most useful genealogical documents first, under a target headed "SELECTED RECORDS". [[claim:m804-selected]]

A real example shows why you read to the end. In Zebulon Applegate's file, his 1832 application starts on page 3. [[claim:nps-applegate]] His widow Rebecca's 1853 pension begins on page 14. [[claim:nps-applegate-widow]] His own handwritten account does not appear until page 32. [[claim:nps-applegate-32]]

A reading order that works:

1. Read the selected records and any summary card or jacket first.
2. Find the earliest declaration and note the act it was filed under.
3. Read every affidavit and note each witness's name and town.
4. Read the widow's or heirs' papers, even if you descend from the veteran.
5. Scan the rest for family-record pages, letters and payment vouchers.

## Step 4: Know which act you are reading

The act behind an application decides what the applicant had to say, so it tells you what the file can contain.

| Act | What it covered | What that means for the file |
|---|---|---|
| 1818 | Continental Line soldiers could apply for federal pensions [[claim:nps-1818]] | Short declarations naming unit and officers |
| 1820 | Added proof of financial need [[claim:nps-1820]] | Property schedules, which often name the wife and children with ages [[claim:m804-property]] |
| 1832 | Six months of service, including militia [[claim:nps-1832]] | Testimony before a Court of Record [[claim:nps-1832-court]], backed by at least one credible witness [[claim:nps-witness]] |
| 1836 | Widows who married the veteran before his last period of service ended (NARA's wording) [[claim:nara-act-1836]] | Proof of marriage, widow's own testimony |
| 1838 | Five-year pensions for widows married after his last service ended and before January 1, 1794, if his service met the 1832 act [[claim:nara-act-1838]] [[claim:stat-1838-after-service]] [[claim:stat-1838-1832-service]] | Marriage date and his service |
| July 1848 | Life pensions for widows married before January 2, 1800 [[claim:nara-act-1848]] | Marriage date is central |
| 1853 and February 1855 | Marriage-date restrictions removed [[claim:nara-act-1853-1855]]: army widows (Act of February 3, 1853, 10 Stat. 154) and navy widows (Act of February 28, 1855, ch. 126, sec. 3, 10 Stat. 616) married after January 1800 [[claim:stat-1853-after-1800]] [[claim:stat-1855feb-1800]] | Later widows' claims |
| 1878 | Widows of soldiers with as few as 14 days of service [[claim:nara-act-1878]] | Very late claims |

Two summaries of the 1836 act do not match word for word. [[claim:nara-act-1836]] NARA's pamphlet says the widow married the veteran before his last period of service expired; the National Park Service summarizes it as marriage before the war ended in 1783. [[claim:nps-1836]]
The Widow Pension Law Checker uses NARA's wording.

## Step 5: Pull out the genealogy

A veteran's application normally gives his age or date of birth, his residence, and sometimes his birthplace. [[claim:m804-veteran-info]]
A widow's application may give her age, residence, maiden name, marriage date and place, and her husband's date and place of death. [[claim:m804-widow-info]]
Family-record pages from Bibles give dates of birth, marriage and death. [[claim:m804-bible-pages]]
Final payment vouchers sometimes give the pensioner's date and place of death and the names of heirs. [[claim:m804-voucher]]
The 1832 declarations answered standard War Department questions, such as where the veteran was living when called into service. [[claim:nps-questions]]
Veterans themselves acknowledged that they might mix up a date or the order of events, so treat old testimony as evidence to test, not as settled fact. [[claim:nps-memory]]
