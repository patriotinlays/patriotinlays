# Same Name, Different Man: Separating Patriots Who Share a Name

::in-short
This guide helps you: prove that a service record with your ancestor's name is your patriot's, not someone else's.
Start by: searching every spelling, then opening every same-name file for the state, not just the first.
Then: use the conflict flag in the [Lineage Evidence Matrix](/tools/lineage-matrix/) for records that disagree.
::end

Finding a service record with your ancestor's name is the start of the work, not the end. Common names repeat across towns and counties, cousins were named for the same grandfather, and record-keepers spelled freely. Lineage reviewers check this, so plan to prove it before you submit.

Points attributed to the 2023 Ohio Society SAR presentation come from that talk, not SAR's national rules.

## How often this goes wrong

A DAR staff genealogist describes sorting six men named Thomas Gillespie in South Carolina. [[claim:darblog-gillespie]]
In the end only four of them had properly sourced service. [[claim:darblog-gillespie-four]]
DAR names multiple people of the same name in an area as a common reason to re-examine a patriot's service. [[claim:darblog-multiple]]
It also checks whether another ancestor of the same name has been established since, to make sure there are two distinct people. [[claim:darblog-same-name]]
A 2023 Ohio Society SAR presentation said weak parent-to-child proof, including unresolved naming conflicts, is among the most common reasons applications are pended. [[claim:ohsar-naming]]

## How the federal files handle same-name veterans

The pension files are arranged by surname. When two or more veterans share a surname and given name, the files are further arranged by the state or organization each man served for. [[claim:m804-same-name]]
Cross-reference cards also exist for variant spellings. [[claim:m804-variant]]
So search every spelling, then open every same-name file for the state, not just the first.

## The disambiguation checklist

Work through these for each candidate record. Every "no" is a reason the record may belong to someone else.

| Test | What to compare | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Residence vs. service | Where your patriot lived and where the service was performed | A residence and service location that do not match is a standard DAR review trigger. [[claim:darblog-residence]] {{asof:sar-apg-patriot-residence}} SAR's application guide says the patriot's residence during the Revolution must agree with the service being claimed. [[claim:sar-apg-patriot-residence]] |
| Distance | Towns, not just counties | DAR's example: two Middlesex County towns nearly 45 miles apart pointed to two men, not one [[claim:darblog-woburn]] |
| Alive at the time | Death date vs. service date | DAR adds a service date for patriots who died during the war, to show the patriot was alive then [[claim:darblog-died]] |
| Right age | Birth range vs. service | {{asof:sar-apg-patriot-age}} SAR's application guide says that if the patriot's birth date can't be found, you cite a proven fact showing the patriot was living at the time of the service and of age for it [[claim:sar-apg-patriot-age]] |
| Same household over time | Age brackets across censuses | Census brackets help track one head of household among same-name men [[claim:nara-census-ballpark]] |
| Same place, several records | Census, deed and court order in one county | NSCDA's South Carolina society's standards say three such records in the same county MAY be enough to show one man [[claim:nscda-same-county]] |
| Unit and officers | Names of officers and companies in each record | A pension application normally states rank, unit and period of service [[claim:m804-rank-unit]] |
| Witnesses and neighbors | Who swore affidavits, who appears on deeds | Compare each witness against known kin and neighbors |

## Watch for these traps

1. Cousins with identical names. NSCDA's South Carolina society's standards give the example of two cousins named Cadwalader Jones, each the son of a different father, in a county whose records burned. [[claim:nscda-cadwalader]]
2. Service in another state. In NARA's Daniel Ide example, the key pension file belonged to a man who served from Massachusetts though the family lived in Vermont, and was easy to pass over for that reason. [[claim:prologue-ide-ma]]
3. A record that only matches on name. A name match with no supporting place, age or kin detail is a lead, not proof. Log it as indirect.
4. Spelling variants. Check the variant-spelling cross-references before deciding a man has no file. [[claim:m804-variant]]

## Build a conflict log

For each candidate man, keep one line per record: the record, the place, the date, the age it implies, and the people it names. Then:

1. Group records that agree on place, age and kin into "Man A", "Man B" and so on.
2. Mark any record that fits more than one man as unresolved.
3. Only attach service to your ancestor when it sits in the same group as a record that ties your patriot to your line.

The Lineage Evidence Matrix has a conflict flag for exactly this. A flagged cell keeps its conflict label until you clear the flag.
