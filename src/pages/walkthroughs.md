# See the tools in action

Sometimes the easiest way to understand a tool is to watch somebody else use it. These two walkthroughs do that, using the same example families you get when you press "Load an example" on the widow checker or "See a filled-in example" on the worksheet. Every name below is made up. The pictures are real captures of the tools, and every quoted result is copied from what the tools actually produced, checked each time the site is built.

## The Widow Pension Law Checker

Imagine you've found a marriage record for a patriot's wife, and you'd like to know whether she could have applied for a pension, and under which law. You don't need to know the laws to start.

Open the [Widow Pension Law Checker](/tools/widow-pension-checker/) and press "Load an example". It fills in a made-up couple who married in May 1777, with his last service ending in March 1778. In the marriage box you'd pick May, type 1777 and leave the day blank, because the record doesn't give one. The last box asks how sure you are; this example uses Exactly. If your own record only says "about 1777", you'd choose About instead, and the tool would show you the wider range it's working with.

::figure widow-date-360.png The marriage date as the example fills it in: month May, year 1777, no day, and Exactly.

Below the dates you'd type 20 months of service, say Yes to the battle question, and leave "What kind of service was it?" as Don't know. [[claim:stat-1855-14-days]] [[claim:nara-act-1832-categories]] Then press "Check the widow pension laws". The "In short" box comes first, as short lines rather than a wall of text. For this couple it reads:

::quote widow-fits Fits: the March 1855 bounty-land and 1878 laws (every rule checked here; other terms not checked)

::quote widow-out Ruled out: the 1838, 1853 and February 1855 laws (by the dates)

::quote widow-open Still possible: the 1836 and 1848 laws (need his kind of service)

Under that, each law gets its own row, and the row always says why. The 1838 row reads "Ruled out by the dates", because that law wanted the marriage to come after his service ended, and this marriage came before. [[claim:stat-1838-after-service]] The March 1855 bounty-land row and the 1878 row read: [[claim:nara-bounty-1855]] [[claim:nara-act-1878]]

::quote widow-fits-row Fits the rules checked here; other terms not checked

That wording matters. It is not a ruling, and it never means the pension is settled. Each of those rows also lists the terms in the law that the tool can't test, such as whether her pension stopped if she married again. [[claim:stat-1878-remarriage]]

::figure widow-summary.png The example's "In short" summary, exactly as the checker prints it.

::figure widow-law-row.png One law row in full: the March 1855 bounty-land law, its rule, and the terms the tool can't check.

The last thing to read is "What to do next". Here it starts with the one answer that would settle the most: his kind of service, which bears on the 1836 and 1848 laws. [[claim:nara-act-1832-categories]] Then it points you to the widow's pension file, which may give her maiden name and the date and place of the marriage. [[claim:m804-widow-info]] The files are online free on FamilySearch, with a login. [[claim:nara-amrev-familysearch]] When you find the file, [How to Read a Pension File](/guides/read-pension-file/) helps you make sense of the papers inside it.

## The Lineage Evidence Matrix

Now imagine the bigger job: you want to see, generation by generation, which links in your line are proven and which still need a record. Open the [worksheet](/tools/lineage-matrix/) and press "See a filled-in example". This family is made up too: a Virginia militiaman named John, and four generations between that patriot and the person applying.

The first step asks for your patriot's name and service. The example gives the patriot's name, Virginia, a county militia unit, and service from May 1777 to October 1779. [[claim:dar-window-core]] Below that it asks how your patriot is related to you, and it fills in the generations in between for you.

::figure worksheet-patriot-360.png The example patriot, with rough service dates. The check below says your patriot's dates fall inside DAR's period for that service, and that it isn't a ruling.

Under the dates you'll see a small result box. For this example it says:

::quote worksheet-dates Your patriot's dates fall inside the period DAR accepts for this type of service. This is not a ruling: your society decides.

The next step is yourself, then your parent, your grandparent, and so on back to your patriot. Generation 1 is you, the applicant. [[claim:dar-gen1-applicant]] Your parents are generation 2, your grandparents generation 3. [[claim:dar-gen-numbering]] For each person, add a rough birth year and state, press "Add a record", and answer the four short questions: what the record shows, what kind of record it is, whether it says it in words or only hints at it, and where you found it. The example's first two generations each have two records filled in, so you can see how the answers look. The best label any record earns is "Evidence Entered — Review Required", because only a society's genealogists decide what's proven. [[claim:dar-review-staff]]

Then comes the part the worksheet is really for: the gaps. For this example family it lists six, in plain words. For instance:

::quote worksheet-gap-1 No death evidence entered yet.

::quote worksheet-gap-2 No birth evidence entered yet.

::quote worksheet-gap-3 Add at least one birth or death record.

Each gap comes with a "Where to look" letter, and each letter is a short piece of advice fitted to that person's era and state. One of them, for the older generations, says that Virginia had no statewide recording of births before 1853, so there's no state birth record to find for that person. [[claim:lva-bmd-pre1853]] The idea is that you never read "something is missing" without also reading where to try next.

::figure worksheet-gaps.png The example's gap list, with the lettered "Where to look" advice underneath.

Last, "What to do next" gathers the same information into a plan: how to prove the patriot's service, which records to find or fix for each generation, and how to reach a local chapter's registrar. DAR's Chapter Locator finds chapters in your area. [[claim:dar-chapter-locator]] For SAR, the next step is finding a sponsor near you. [[claim:sar-sponsor-registrar]] Open "my printable report" to print it or save it as a PDF, and take that with you when you talk to the registrar.

One thing worth saying plainly: this example family has gaps on purpose. A worksheet full of green boxes wouldn't teach you much. Your own list will look different, and it will shrink as you find records. If you'd rather start with your own family, press "Delete all my worksheet data" on the tool page to clear the example, then fill in your patriot and start the generations with yourself.
